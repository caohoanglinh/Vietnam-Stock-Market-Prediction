Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
Background
Vietnam is a country rich in history, culture, and natural beauty — home to stunning
destinations like Ha Long Bay, the ancient city of Hue, and countless hidden gems tucked
away in bustling cities and remote villages. Each year, millions of tourists are drawn to
explore these diverse landmarks. As tourism continues to thrive, so does the need for
smart technologies that can enhance how travelers discover and engage with the places
they visit.
One promising area of innovation is landmark recognition: the ability for a system to
identify landmarks from photos and provide relevant information. Imagine being able to
snap a photo of a temple or statue and instantly learn about its history, significance, or lo-
cation. This kind of experience not only makes traveling more convenient and interactive,
but also encourages deeper cultural appreciation.
However, building a system that can accurately recognize landmarks — especially
in a place like Vietnam — isn’t easy. Many landmarks may look visually similar or
have limited online documentation. Solving this problem calls for advanced computer
vision methods that go beyond general-purpose image recognition, with a specific focus
on local, domain-specific knowledge.
1.2
Problem statement
As more travelers explore Vietnam, the demand for quick and easy access to reliable
landmark information grows. However, many tourists, particularly international visitors,
often struggle to identify unfamiliar sites or find trustworthy details in real time. While
traditional travel apps and guidebooks are helpful, they don’t always offer the precision or
immediacy needed when navigating a complex, visually rich environment like Vietnam.
Current image recognition tools are typically built on broad, global datasets and
aren’t optimized for local nuances. Landmarks that look similar or are poorly docu-
mented can confuse even state-of-the-art systems. Moreover, these models usually re-
quire large amounts of labeled training data — something not always available for every
Vietnamese landmark. This creates a gap between what existing technology can offer
and what tourists actually need on the ground.

1.3
Solution approach
To bridge this gap, I propose a landmark recognition system tailored specifically for
Vietnamese tourism. I explore a range of approaches — from traditional CNNs to Few-
Shot Learning, and ultimately, a landmark retrieval system using a vector database —
enabling fast and accurate similarity searches based on image content.
To ensure the system performs well even with limited data, I apply FSL techniques.
This allows the model to recognize new landmarks from just a handful of support images,
making it more adaptable and scalable. Additionally, the system supports multimodal
inputs — combining image and text embeddings — so users can search by either taking
a photo or describing the landmark, offering greater flexibility.
The end goal is to provide travelers with a simple, intuitive tool: take a photo of a
landmark and instantly receive rich, contextual information — without relying on GPS or
manually searching online. By combining advanced AI with practical travel needs, this
solution aims to make exploring Vietnam smarter, easier, and more enriching.
1.4
Motivation and Objectives
Motivation
While landmark recognition has been widely explored in global contexts, there is still a
noticeable lack of research and practical applications focused specifically on Vietnam.
As someone passionate about both artificial intelligence and Vietnamese culture, I am
personally motivated to contribute to this underexplored area. By developing a system
that can effectively recognize landmarks in Vietnam, I hope to support both local tourism
and technological advancement, making cultural exploration more accessible to domestic
and international travelers alike.
Objectives
The main objective of this project is to develop an intelligent landmark recognition sys-
tem tailored for Vietnamese tourism. This includes exploring different approaches—from
CNN to FSL and landmark retrieval—to find the optimal solution, and then integrating
the recognition system into a user-friendly chatbot powered by an LLM.

Chapter 2
Dataset
2.1
Overview
This chapter provides an overview of the dataset used in the Vietnam Landmark Recogni-
tion project during my internship. The dataset serves as the basis for training and evaluat-
ing computer vision and multimodal models aimed at identifying Vietnamese landmarks
from images and text queries. It consists of images representing 45 notable landmarks
across various cities in Vietnam, along with metadata including landmark names, city
locations, and textual descriptions.
During the early stage of the project, a smaller dataset was used specifically for train-
ing and evaluating CNN models in a standard image classification setup. This dataset
allowed me to observe and validate the effectiveness of using deep learning techniques
for landmark recognition.
The final, larger dataset was carefully curated and preprocessed to ensure high-quality
and diverse inputs suitable for few-shot learning and retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) systems. It was used for training few-shot learning models, generating image
and text embeddings, and indexing vectors into a vector database. This dataset played
a crucial role in building the Vietnam Landmark Recognition system, enabling robust
retrieval and recognition capabilities.
2.2
Data collection
The data used in this project was collected through two main approaches: self-capturing
and online sourcing.
Self-captured data: In the early stage of the project, images were collected man-
ually by visiting landmark locations and capturing photos or recording videos on-site.
However, the process was time-consuming and resource-intensive, particularly due to the
need to travel to each location and preprocess the video data into usable image frames.
As a result, this approach was discontinued after the initial five landmarks.
Online data collection: To scale the dataset more efficiently and improve its general-
izability, I shifted to downloading publicly available images from the internet. These im-
ages were sourced from travel websites, image search engines, and open-source datasets.
This approach enabled a broader and faster accumulation of high-quality images repre-

Chapter 3
Methodology
3.1
Overview
This chapter outlines the experimental methods explored in the development of the Viet-
nam Landmark Recognition system. The goal was to identify the most effective approach
for accurately recognizing Vietnamese landmarks from images. A series of experiments
were conducted, each building on the insights of the previous, to evaluate the strengths
and limitations of various techniques in landmark classification.
The methodology followed a progressive exploration of the following approaches:
1. CNN Classification: As a baseline, a standard CNN was trained to classify Viet-
namese landmarks using supervised learning. This provided an initial performance
benchmark and highlighted the challenges of traditional classification with limited
data per class.
2. Few-Shot Learning: To improve generalization with scarce training data, a Proto-
typical Network was implemented. This method enabled classification by compar-
ing image embeddings to class prototypes in an embedding space, offering better
adaptability to unseen classes.
3. LLM Classification with Prompts: Gemini [3], a multimodal foundation model,
was tested with prompt-based classification. This experiment leveraged Gemini’s
ability to understand both images and text, aiming to improve recognition using
high-level semantic prompts.
4. Landmark Retrieval using Vector Database : Inspired by the prototypical net-
work concept, this experiment stored image and text embeddings in a vector database.
Cosine similarity was used for retrieval, allowing flexible and scalable classifica-
tion based on similarity search rather than fixed labels.
5. LLM Integration: In the final stage, Gemini was integrated as a chatbot interface
to verify and explain retrieval results from the vector database. This added a con-
versational layer that enhances user interaction and trust through natural language
explanations and broader contextual knowledge.

3.2
Models
3.2.1
Convolutional Neural Networks
CNNs are a class of deep learning models particularly effective for processing and an-
alyzing visual data. They operate by applying convolutional filters to input images to
automatically learn hierarchical features, starting from low-level edges and textures to
high-level semantic representations. A typical CNN architecture consists of convolu-
tional layers, pooling layers to reduce dimensionality, and fully connected layers for
classification or embedding generation [4].
In this project, several CNN architectures were utilized to extract image features:
• VGG16 [5]: A deep network with 16 layers, VGG16 is known for its simplicity
and uniform architecture, using small 3×3 filters across the convolutional layers.
Although computationally heavy, it provides strong performance on image classi-
fication tasks and serves as a solid baseline for feature extraction.
• ResNet18 and ResNet50 [6]: These are residual networks that incorporate short-
cut connections to allow gradients to flow directly across layers, addressing the
vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. ResNet18 is a lighter version with
18 layers, suitable for lower-resource environments, while ResNet50 is deeper and
more powerful, providing richer representations.
• EfficientNet-B1 [7]: This is a more recent architecture that scales model depth,
width, and resolution in a balanced way. EfficientNet-B1 offers a good trade-off
between accuracy and efficiency, making it suitable for applications that require
both high performance and fast inference times.
3.2.2
Few-Shot Learning
FSL is a paradigm in machine learning that aims to enable models to recognize new
categories from only a small number of examples. This is particularly valuable in real-
world scenarios where collecting and annotating large amounts of data is impractical.
Figure 3.1: An illustration of the Prototypical Network, demonstrating how a
query data point is classified based on its distance to the class prototypes.

The model employed for FSL in this project is the ProtoNet [8]. This method assumes
that each class can be represented by a prototype, which is the mean of the support
samples in an embedding space. During inference, query images are embedded into the
same space, and classification is performed by finding the nearest class prototype using
a distance metric, typically Euclidean or cosine similarity. This approach is simple yet
effective, especially in few-shot settings where data is limited.
3.2.3
Vision Transformer
The ViT [9] is a model architecture that adapts the transformer structure, originally de-
veloped for natural language processing, to visual data. Instead of processing the image
as a whole or through convolutions, ViT splits an image into fixed-size patches, flat-
tens them, and treats each patch as a token. These tokens are passed through multiple
transformer layers, allowing the model to capture long-range dependencies and contex-
tual relationships between different regions of the image. ViT has demonstrated strong
performance, especially when trained on large datasets, by leveraging global attention
mechanisms instead of local convolutions.
Figure 3.2: An illustration of the ViT, demonstrating how an image is classi-
fied through the Transformer Encoder layers.
3.2.4
Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training
CLIP [2], developed by OpenAI, is a multimodal model designed to learn visual concepts
from natural language supervision. It consists of two separate encoders: one for images
(in this project, a ViT backbone is used) and one for text. Both inputs are encoded into
a shared embedding space where their similarity can be directly computed. The model
is trained using a contrastive loss that aligns matching image-text pairs and separates
non-matching pairs. The similarity function used is:

Large Language Model
Gemini Flash 2.0, developed by Google, is a lightweight yet high-performance large lan-

Few-Shot Learning with ProtoNet
This is the next stage after I found many disadvantages of CNN, which will be discussed
more in Chapter 4. FSL is adapted due to the data-hungry nature of CNNs and the low
quantity of images in my dataset. My thought is that although a landmark can be captured
from many directions and angles, they should form a cluster, and the mean vector helps
ensure that if the query image is from a different angle, it can still be recognized. My
preprocessing requirements (Table 2.1) helped ensure a quality embedding space, and
the dataset visualization (Figure 2.4) proved my thought.
I tested with 4 backbones: EfficientNet-B1, ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and ViT. Episodic
training is used for this type of model. I set up 200 tasks per epoch; each task contains
n ways and k shots, which means each task has n classes and k examples per class. For
each task, a random support set and query set are fed to the model. Due to this special
training method, the dataset needs to have more classes, which is why Dataset v2 is used:
29 classes for finetuning and 16 classes for testing. During finetuning, the backbone is
frozen and only the embedding space is trained. The loss function used is softmax over

3.3.4
Landmark Retrieval with Vector Database
This is the classification system that I designed for scalability. It applies the concept of
ProtoNet, but its embedding space is replaced by a vector database. After the image is
embedded, a mean vector is calculated to represent the class. It is then stored in a vector
database instead of an embedding space.
The database I used is Milvus, which is an open-source, high-performance vector
database built for scale. I also create a text description for each landmark to enable later
implementations. I used ChatGPT to generate text descriptions that are best suited for
cosine similarity. Then I verified all the texts by comparing side-by-side the text and
the image of the same landmark, and I also ensured that the text description for each
landmark is unique. Both the images and text are encoded by CLIP, then the encoded
data along with metadata — landmark name and city — are inserted into the database.
Figure 3.5: An illustration of how the data is inserted to the database.
For the retrieval task, approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search with a cosine sim-
ilarity metric is used. The ANN search algorithm uses an index file that records the
sorted order of vector embeddings to quickly locate a subgroup probably containing vec-
tor embeddings most similar to the query vector. From that, four searching methods are
implemented:
• Search with image: The query image is encoded by CLIP, then an ANN search is
performed and returns the top 5 landmark names.
• Search with text: Similar to image search, but the input is replaced by text.

Figure 3.6: An illustration of how search with image/text is perform.
• Hybrid search: Uses both image and text to query, then applies a reranking strat-
egy to return the final top 5 landmark names. There are two reranking strategies in
Milvus: Weighted Ranker and RRFRanker. The Weighted Ranker strategy merges
results by calculating a weighted score of similarity scores from different vector
searches, and the RRFRanker combines results based on ranking that balances the
ranks of results from different searches. The default rerank method in my imple-
mentation is RRFRanker.
Figure 3.7: An illustration of how hybrid search is perform.
• Filtered search: Filters the database using metadata (city), then performs one of
the three search methods above. The default search method here is search with
image.
These search methods help enhance the accuracy of the system while maintaining
speed. For the support set, I created two variants: 10 support images per class and 20
images per class, to evaluate how the size of the support set affects system performance.
The query set contains 2,264 images from 45 non-overlapping classes. This helps me
assess how the number of support images influences performance.
I also performed repeated sampling with evaluation, which means that from the orig-
inal dataset, I randomly selected 10 or 20 support images per class, then used the remain-
ing images as the query set. This allows me to observe how the quality and variability of
the support set affect performance.

4.2
Comparisons
4.2.1
Comparison of tested methods
In the early stage of the project, I evaluated CNN models using Dataset V1, which was
limited to 5 classes with a relatively balanced number of images. This allowed for con-
trolled experiments in supervised classification. However, once Dataset V2 was com-
pleted—with 45 classes and a significant class imbalance (ranging from 30 to 153 images
per class)—I did not re-evaluate CNNs on this larger dataset. CNNs typically require bal-
anced and abundant training data to perform well, and applying them directly to Dataset
V2 would risk biased learning toward majority classes.
More importantly, Dataset V2 reflects a realistic real-world scenario, where some
landmarks are well documented while others have only a few available images. This
highlights the need for methods that are inherently more robust to data imbalance and
scalable without retraining. Based on its strong performance in the experiments, the
Landmark Retrieval system using a Vector Database was selected as the final solution.

Chapter 5
Conclusions and Future Work
5.1
Conclusion
Throughout this internship, I created a Vietnam Landmark dataset. Although the dataset
is imbalanced, it remains a valuable custom resource that supported the development
of the system. After testing various methods, I successfully implemented a Vietnam
Landmark Recognition system that leverages the strengths of CNNs (speed), FSL (data
efficiency), and LLMs (reasoning and broad knowledge). The system is scalable thanks to
the use of a vector database instead of storing embeddings directly in memory. Although
only image-based search is currently integrated into the chatbot, the system also supports
text-based, city-filtered, and hybrid search through the database. This demonstrates the
flexibility and extensibility of the architecture.
However, while the integration of the LLM helps verify answers, it also increases
latency. This represents a trade-off between speed and accuracy. To address this, I plan
to increase the amount of data so the system becomes less reliant on the LLM.
5.2
Reflection
I started from scratch—beginning with CNNs, identifying their limitations such as being
data-hungry, and applying FSL to address those issues. Although FSL helped mitigate
the data scarcity problem, challenges like the need for retraining when introducing new
classes and limited scalability remained. To overcome this, I replaced the embedding
space with a vector database, which made the system more scalable. I also leveraged the
knowledge of a LLM to improve accuracy and handle queries involving images that are
not related or out of the database.
This project gave me hands-on experience with deep learning, vector databases, and
LLM integration in a real-world application. Tackling challenges such as handling im-
balanced data and aligning multimodal embeddings provided valuable learning opportu-
nities.

Future work
There are several directions for improving and expanding my system:
